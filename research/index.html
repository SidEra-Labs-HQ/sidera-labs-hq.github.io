Research

Six areas, and what we can honestly say about each.

These are the fields SidEra Labs is interested in. For each one you will find what it investigates, why it matters, the questions that are still open, and a plain statement of what has actually been published. Today that statement is the same for every area: nothing yet.

Status of all six areas

Every area below is Exploratory. SidEra Labs has not published research, results or projects in any of them. The sections describe the field and the questions that make it worth studying. They do not describe completed programs.

01Exploratory

Artificial Intelligence and Intelligent Systems

Machine learning models do not run alone. They sit inside products, pipelines and decisions. This area looks at how learned systems behave in that setting.

What it investigates

How models learn from data, how they generalise or fail to, and how they are combined with conventional software into systems that make or support decisions. That includes language and multimodal models, retrieval, evaluation, and the data and tooling around them.

Why it matters

Impressive benchmark scores can hide brittle behaviour. Whether a model is useful depends on its failure modes, its cost, and whether the surrounding system can notice and contain mistakes.

Important questions

  • How should a model be evaluated for the job it will actually do, rather than for a public benchmark?
  • Where does performance fall apart: rare inputs, shifted data, long contexts, adversarial text?
  • What belongs in the model and what belongs in ordinary, testable code?
  • How can uncertainty be reported in a way a downstream system can use?

Possible applications

  • Decision support with clear confidence reporting
  • Search and retrieval over technical material
  • Classification and extraction pipelines with audit trails

02Exploratory

Software Engineering and Developer Infrastructure

Good ideas survive or die in the build, test and release machinery around them. This area studies that machinery.

What it investigates

How software is structured, built, tested, released and observed: architecture, build systems, continuous integration, dependency management, reproducibility, and the tools developers use every day. It also covers how AI assistance changes that workflow.

Why it matters

Most technical debt is invisible until it is expensive. Reproducible builds and fast, trustworthy tests are what let a small team change a system without fear. Machine learning systems add their own debt on top, in data, configuration and glue code.

Important questions

  • What is the cheapest test suite that still catches the failures that matter?
  • How can builds and experiments be made reproducible by default?
  • What should be automated, and what should stay a human review?
  • How do code-writing tools change review, testing and ownership?

Possible applications

  • Reproducible environments for research code
  • Lightweight pipelines for static and small services
  • Practical standards for testing and documentation

03Exploratory

Agent Systems and Intelligent Automation

An agent is software that repeatedly observes, decides and acts. The loop is simple to draw and hard to make dependable.

What it investigates

Systems in which a model chooses actions, calls tools and reacts to results over many steps. The area covers control loops, tool design, state and memory, planning, verification, cost and time budgets, and how to test behaviour that is not fully predictable.

Why it matters

As systems gain the ability to act, mistakes stop being wrong text and become wrong actions. Reliable automation needs boundaries, checks that the loop cannot talk its way past, and a record of what happened.

Important questions

  • When is a fixed workflow better than an open-ended agent?
  • How should tools be specified so that mistakes are cheap and recoverable?
  • What stop conditions and budgets prevent runaway loops?
  • How should untrusted content in tool results be handled?

Possible applications

  • Task automation with explicit approval points
  • Code and data maintenance workflows with automatic checks
  • Monitoring and triage with a human decision at the end

04Exploratory

Advanced Computing

What is practical to compute depends on hardware shape, memory movement and numeric precision as much as on algorithms.

What it investigates

How computation maps onto real hardware: parallel and accelerated processors, memory hierarchies, numeric formats, scheduling, and emerging approaches such as new accelerator designs. The focus is on understanding and measuring, not on building chips.

Why it matters

For many modern workloads the limit is moving data, not arithmetic. Knowing where time and energy go lets engineers make large improvements with modest changes, and avoid optimising the wrong thing.

Important questions

  • Where does a given workload spend its time and energy: compute, memory or communication?
  • How much numeric precision does a task need, and what does lowering it cost?
  • How do results change across different hardware?
  • What is a fair way to compare performance claims?

Possible applications

  • Profiling and performance studies on commodity hardware
  • Efficient inference for small models
  • Clear benchmarking practice for reproducible comparisons

05Exploratory

Robotics and Hardware-Integrated Engineering

Software meets physics here: sensors drift, motors saturate, clocks disagree, and the world does not wait for a retry.

What it investigates

Systems that sense and act in physical environments: sensing and state estimation, control, kinematics, embedded software, timing, simulation, and the interface between software teams and hardware. It also covers the practical craft of getting a prototype to run reliably on a bench.

Why it matters

A physical system adds constraints that pure software ignores: real-time deadlines, noise, wear and safety. Many failures come from mismatched units, frames or timing at an interface, not from the clever parts.

Important questions

  • How should interfaces between software and hardware state units, frames, timing and limits?
  • What is the safe state when something fails, and is it tested?
  • How far can simulation be trusted before hardware is involved?
  • What should be logged so that a failure can be replayed?

Possible applications

  • Sensor-driven prototypes and bench rigs
  • Control and monitoring software for small devices
  • Simulation-first development with hardware-in-the-loop checks

06Exploratory

Applied and Experimental Research

This is the method that connects the other five areas: small, well-posed experiments with honest reporting.

What it investigates

How to turn an idea into a test and a test into evidence. It covers experiment design, baselines, ablations, statistical care, replication and reporting, and the discipline of publishing negative results. It is where the other areas are checked against reality.

Why it matters

Without method, results are anecdotes. Careful design is what separates a finding from a coincidence, and it is cheap compared with building on a mistaken result.

Important questions

  • What is the simplest baseline that could explain this result?
  • How many repeated runs are needed before a difference means something?
  • Which claims have been reproduced, and which only observed once?
  • How should limitations be written so that they are read?

Possible applications

  • Evaluation protocols for projects in the other areas
  • Replication write-ups of published methods
  • Prototypes accompanied by a clear record of tests
